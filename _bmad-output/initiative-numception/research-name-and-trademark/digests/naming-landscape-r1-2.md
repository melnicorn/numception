# Digest: Naming landscape for math/number puzzle games (r1-2)

Decision served: choosing a distinctive, available name for a new math puzzle game (find the a op b = c triple hidden among numbers in geometric shapes; nested tiers; fractal/pyramid endless mode; neon arcade and zen modes).
Run date: 2026-10-08. Method: web search/fetch only (12 tool calls). Class key: fact = documented primary/official; secondary = reputable secondary; anecdotal = blog/interview.

## Findings

### Q2: The KenKen / Calcudoku / Mathdoku precedent

1. **Claim:** Japanese math teacher Tetsuya Miyamoto invented KenKen in 2004. Nextoy, LLC holds trademarks on "KenKen" and "KenDoku" as names for brain-training puzzles. "Calcudoku" and "Mathdoku" are sometimes used by people who lack rights to those marks. The Times (London) began publishing the puzzles in March 2008.
   - source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/KenKen | publisher: Wikipedia | pub_date: n/a (living page) | accessed: 2026-10-08 | confidence: high | class: secondary
2. **Claim:** Calcudoku.org states that "KenKen" and "KenDoku" are trademarks of Nextoy LLC and says it is not affiliated with Nextoy. The site lists other names used for the same puzzle type, including Newdoku and Keen.
   - source: https://calcudoku.org | publisher: calcudoku.org (hobby site) | pub_date: n/a | accessed: 2026-10-08 | confidence: medium (search-snippet level, page not fetched) | class: secondary
3. **Claim:** Robert Fuhrer of Nextoy says he paid for the effort so the trademark would be held in his name ("KenKen would be registered under my name"). The registration covered several categories and was pursued in the US and other countries, which brought ongoing renewal and maintenance fees. A Japanese editor first called the puzzle "Ken Ken Blocks." This article says "ken" means wisdom; Wikipedia says it means cleverness (賢).
   - source: https://hey.gg/blog/kenken | publisher: hey.gg blog (Fuhrer account) | pub_date: conflicting (body says 2024-01-02, metadata says 2026-10-07) | accessed: 2026-10-08 | confidence: medium | class: anecdotal
4. **Claim:** The puzzle mechanic itself is free to use. Competitors used it under generic portmanteau names (Calcudoku, Mathdoku), and a current App Store app is titled "Mathdoku - Daily Challenge." The lesson: the mark protects the name, not the mechanic, so a generic or descriptive name is weak and crowded.
   - source: https://apps.apple.com/app/id6752537643 (app listing); https://blog.tanyakhovanova.com/?p=1048 (blogger: you can publish KenKen puzzles as long as you don't call them KenKen) | publisher: Apple App Store; Tanya Khovanova blog | pub_date: n/a | accessed: 2026-10-08 | confidence: medium (inference built on snippets) | class: anecdotal / inference
5. **Claim:** "Sudoku" is a shortening of Nikoli's 1984 title *Sūji wa dokushin ni kagiru* and is a registered trademark in Japan. Partly because of this, the generic "Number Place" (Japanese: Nanpure) is still used. Dell published the puzzle as "Number Place" (commonly dated 1979).
   - source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sudoku | publisher: Wikipedia | pub_date: n/a | accessed: 2026-10-08 | confidence: medium-high | class: secondary
6. **Claim:** "Kakuro" abbreviates *kasan kurosu* ("addition cross"). The original English name was "Cross Sums," coined in 1966 by Jacob E. Funk of Dell Magazines. "Cross Addition," "Kakkuro" and "Kakoro" are variants. Wikipedia does not mention any trademark on "Kakuro."
   - source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kakuro | publisher: Wikipedia | pub_date: n/a | accessed: 2026-10-08 | confidence: high | class: secondary

### Q1: Naming patterns

7. **Claim:** Nerdle (launched 20 Jan 2022 by a London data scientist and family) is the main math take on Wordle. It spawned variant names on its own site (Mini, Micro, Maxi, Bi, Quad, Instant, Speed Nerdle). The "-le" suffix spread widely in 2022 (Worldle, Flagle, Quordle, Squirdle, Taylordle, Primel).
   - source: https://nerdlegame.com/hq/math-wordle.html; https://dailyhive.com/toronto/wordle-spin-off-word-games | publisher: Nerdle HQ; Daily Hive | pub_date: 2022 (lists) | accessed: 2026-10-08 | confidence: medium-high | class: primary (Nerdle) / secondary
8. **Pattern synthesis (inference from findings 1-7, not a sourced claim):** Three naming families stand out:
   - Japanese-derived compounds (Sudoku, Kakuro, KenKen), often short reduplicated or clipped words.
   - Generic "-doku" portmanteaus (Calcudoku, Mathdoku, KenDoku, Newdoku) that sprang up around trademark walls.
   - The "-le" suffix after Wordle (Nerdle, Primel).

   The generic forms are crowded and hard to protect. An invented coinage with a Japanese feel is the pattern that produced the protectable marks (Sudoku in Japan, KenKen).
   - confidence: medium | class: inference

### Q3: Practical availability checks

9. **Claim:** Apple App Review Guideline 2.3.7 says to "Choose a unique app name," limits app names to 30 characters, and bars stuffing metadata with trademarked terms or popular app names. Guideline 4.1(a) bars making "minor changes to another app's name." Guideline 4.1(c) bars using another developer's brand or product name in your app name or icon without approval. Guideline 5.2.1 bars using third-party trademarks without permission.
   - source: https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/ | publisher: Apple | pub_date: n/a (current) | accessed: 2026-10-08 | confidence: high | class: fact
10. **Claim:** Google Play metadata policy: "Your app title must be 30 characters or less." Titles may not contain emojis or repeated special characters. ALL CAPS is discouraged unless it is part of the brand. Titles may not indicate ranking, price or promotions ("#1", "free for limited time only"). One ASO source says the limit dropped from 50 to 30 characters, effective 29 Sep 2021.
    - source: https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/9898842/ | publisher: Google | pub_date: n/a (current) | accessed: 2026-10-08 | confidence: high | class: fact
11. **Claim:** Apple search results often truncate titles. One ASO guide says to about 26 characters, so the distinctive part of the name should come first.
    - source: https://www.apptweak.com/en/aso-blog/app-name-guidelines-and-best-practices | publisher: AppTweak | pub_date: n/a | accessed: 2026-10-08 | confidence: low-medium | class: secondary
12. **Claim:** The USPTO trademark search hub points to the Trademark Search system (tmsearch.uspto.gov), the Trademark ID Manual, a "Using coordinated classes" page and a likelihood-of-confusion page. Its comprehensive-clearance guidance says to search for marks "confusingly similar to yours" used with the same or related goods and services. It also says to check the Trademark Official Gazette and to search for unregistered common-law use (internet, plus state trademark and business registries for all 50 states, D.C. and Puerto Rico). For a deeper search it suggests a screening service or a US-licensed attorney.
    - source: https://www.uspto.gov/trademarks/search ; https://www.uspto.gov/trademarks/search/comprehensive-clearance-search-similar-trademarks | publisher: USPTO | pub_date: n/a | accessed: 2026-10-08 | confidence: high | class: fact
13. **Claim:** A game brand typically spans three Nice classes:
    - Class 9: game software and downloadable games.
    - Class 28: physical and electronic games, board and card games.
    - Class 41: online gaming and entertainment services.

    Filers commonly cover all three. UK Trade Mark Journal entries show this 9/28/41 pattern.
    - source: https://gleanmark.com/nice-class/28 ; https://legalmoveslawfirm.com/trademark-class-9/ ; ipo.gov.uk journal entries | publisher: GleanMark; Legal Moves Law Firm; UK IPO | pub_date: various | accessed: 2026-10-08 | confidence: medium | class: secondary
